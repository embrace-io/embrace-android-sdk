Deciding whether a benchmark difference is real

My reading of the performance-evaluation literature, and what I think it means for how we compare runs. The short version: the hard questions are not which test. They are what counts as one observation — which we had been answering wrong — and which problems a larger sample can actually solve, which is fewer than it looks.

No statistics background needed. Every term used here is defined in plain language in the glossary at the end — start there if "clustering" or "p-value" is not already familiar.

Status: what running these methods has taught, 2026-08-17 → 08-26

This brief was written into a world of one pass per arm. The standing design is now 10 passes × 20 iterations, and everything below was earned by a specific incident or result rather than reasoned out in advance. Where an entry corrects something stated earlier in this document, it says so.

What is now standing practice

  • The test pair is the cluster bootstrap for the interval and the cluster permutation for p, both at G = 10. The permutation floor is 2/C(20,10) ≈ 1.1×10⁻⁵, against 0.029 at the old G = 4 — several now-published results at p = 5×10⁻⁵ were structurally unreportable before the shape change.
  • When the bootstrap CI and the permutation p disagree, report "suggestive", not "supported". Seen live at p = 0.014 with a CI of [−16.24, +0.27] — the signature of large between-pass variance. The CI-excludes-zero criterion is the stricter gate and is the one the published tables verdict on.
  • Judge precision by the CI half-width on the arm median — never by IQR. An early read used IQR over raw launches to ask whether the shape change bought precision and concluded it had not; IQR measures launch-to-launch spread, which was never the claim. On the right statistic the half-width fell 45–95%.
  • Validity precedes statistics. Two guards, each of which caught a silent corruption: verify every leg against the device crash buffer rather than the harness exit code — one device produced 200 green, complete, plausible traces from a process that crashed on every launch — and refuse pass-level analysis when n ≠ G×m, because positionally-recovered pass boundaries misalign on any dropped trace.
  • No inference shares the host with a live leg. A quiet host is part of the measurement recipe, so bootstrap and permutation runs are queued for ingest gaps — even when that means published tables carry explicit pending cells. A pending cell beats a number computed under contention.

Corrections to what this document says above

  • "Between-pass is 93–99% of the variance" describes the ARM ESTIMATE, not a launch — and it is per device/version, not a fleet constant. Two decompositions are easy to conflate, and one nearly reached publication as a refutation of the other: the raw ICC (the share of a single launch's variance that is between-cluster) versus the split of σ²between/G + σ²within/(G·n), which is what run shape is chosen on and whose second term carries a 1/n the raw components do not. On the same 200 launches the flagship gives raw ICC 0.027 and an estimator split of 36% between / 64% within — while the 93–99% on record is the estimator split measured on the A14 at 4×50. Both are right for their own cell. So "spend the budget on passes, not iterations" does not transfer universally: where the within-pass term still carries most of the estimate's variance, iterations are not wasted. Always say which decomposition a number is, and compute it per arm rather than inheriting it.
  • Interleaving is the standard for cheap-toggle arms; version arms run block-sequential, on purpose. The advice above to "interleave arms rather than running them in blocks" holds for factors that switch via runtime state — a device setting, pm compile, a seeded file. An SDK-version switch is a pin flip, a rebuild and a byte-different APK install, and installs drive the compile-parity state machine, so pass-level version interleaving would entangle the effect with install-cycle state. The sequential design substitutes 10 independent installs per arm, cool gates re-normalising thermal state per pass, and an empirical bound on the drift interleaving would have cancelled: the same arm re-measured across days agrees to ~1.5%. The line: those bounds carry 10–45% effects; a claimed version effect in the few-percent range deserves interleaved confirmation despite the parity cost.
  • Run shape is series-defining, not a neutral repackaging. Changing it moved medians device-dependently (Pixel 3 −10 to −19%, A14 +10 to +17%). Within-pass drift is real and replicates with a per-device mechanism, but quantitatively explains only 7–37% of the shift. Treat run_shape like instrument: part of the recipe key, never comparable across values.
  • A corrected test can still be the wrong test. The section-share drift discriminator survived Benjamini–Hochberg mechanically and then failed its own premise in both directions. FDR fixes the error rate, not the hypothesis.

Limits discovered by running into them

  • Tail statistics follow a power gradient — median → p90 → p95 — and that must be reported as power, not inconsistency. Across the same seven comparisons: 5/7 supported at the median, 5/7 at p90, 3/7 at p95, with zero sign flips above noise. One instructive inversion — a comparison suggestive at the median but supported at p90 — shows that with large variance, which statistic clears the bar can come down to where the variance concentrates.
  • A quiet campaign cannot explain variance, and that is a fact about the design rather than about the explanatory variables. Joining 28 production attributes to 200 launches found nothing significant at either level, because a cool-gated, settled, single-device run held thermal headroom to a 2.8-point range and never fired low-memory once. The same attributes correlate strongly when state is deliberately ramped. Design the run to vary what you intend to explain, or the analysis is uninformative by construction rather than by finding.
  • A null result is only meaningful if the arms provably differ — and "provably" is harder than it looks. (2026-08-26) If a config flag silently fails to apply, both arms are the same program and the analysis returns "no significant difference" with clean statistics and nothing anywhere distinguishing that from a real null. The obvious artefact checks do not settle it: identical APK size is what a one-opcode boolean injection should produce, the digest moves for zip metadata alone, and Android builds are not byte-reproducible — the same two trees differed by 275 dex byte positions built as a pair and by 11,790 after independent rebuilds, a 43× swing that is entirely noise. Only byte-identical dex is conclusive, and only of failure. The practical control is to design the A/B so it re-measures something already established independently — reproducing a known median inside the new run is what licenses reading the new statistic.
  • Reduce for the question after next. (2026-08-26) Keeping per-launch values is usually framed as a precision matter; it is also a survivability one. Two campaigns computed medians only, were never ingested into a store, and had their traces purged — so when the obvious follow-up arrived ("does the effect hold at p95?"), it was not merely unanswered but unanswerable, and needed the whole A/B re-run. Store the per-launch array: it is small, and it is the only artefact from which any future statistic can still be computed.
  • Structure and level need different sample sizes. (2026-08-26) Which trace sections nest inside which is a property of the code path and is stable across a handful of traces; a duration median is not. Taking both from the same four traces produced a section longer than the window enclosing it — 100.77 ms against 94.46 — because the sample came from the head of the pass most distorted by install aftermath.

Samples fix variance. They never fix bias.

This is the distinction the rest of the document rests on, and it settles most arguments about "just run it more times". A confounder is bias. Collecting more data under the same confounded skew produces a tighter interval around the wrong number — a confident wrong answer, which is more dangerous than an obviously uncertain one because it invites action.

So "more samples" only answers a problem when the thing being fought is random with respect to the comparison. Three questions decide that, and they are worth asking in order before adding any data at all.

1. Is the variation random with respect to the arms, or systematic?

If every pass of arm A ran hot and every pass of B ran cold, more passes measure the temperature difference more precisely and say nothing about the arms. No sample size repairs this.

The fix is not statistical. It is randomisation or blocking, and its purpose is to convert a systematic difference into a random one — at which point replication finally has something it can average away. That, and not tidiness, is what interleaving arms is for.

  • Production gets this from a randomised percentage rollout: random assignment of devices balances confounders nobody named or measured. That is strictly stronger than stratification, which can only handle the confounders you thought of. Restricting to one SoC is then a precision play, not a validity one.
  • Benchmarks get it from interleaving arms, counterbalancing order across devices, and balancing or pinning known two-state confounders. A benchmark is not purely "controlled" — it controls what it can, randomises what it cannot, and replicates to shrink the residue.

2. At which level does the variation actually live?

Replication only averages away variation at the level being replicated. Adding iterations inside a pass reduces iteration-level noise and touches nothing else — which is why a device at ρ = 0.67 turned 200 iterations into an effective six. Adding passes reduces pass-level variance. Match the repetition to the level that carries the variance, and measure which level that is rather than assuming.

3. Is the confounder continuous, or a discrete switch?

This one is easy to miss and expensive to get wrong. Replication converges as 1/√n, which is weak medicine against a bimodal confounder.

Install-time compile parity is a two-state switch worth about ±10% around the mean. With G independent installs per arm the parity-induced error on one arm is 10%/√G, and on a difference between two arms it is √2 × 10%/√G:

Pushing it below 2% by replication alone would take about fifty installs per arm, which is never going to happen. Balancing it deliberately — two installs of each state per arm, or pinning with pm compile — removes it outright. For a discrete confounder, blocking beats any feasible amount of replication.

Stop adding runs when the residual random error sits comfortably below the effect worth caring about, or when uncontrolled bias exceeds what any sample size could fix — whichever comes first. Past that point the spend buys precision on a biased estimate.

The mistake worth avoiding

Given two sets of launch times, the tempting move is a t-test on the pooled iterations. That is wrong here for three independent reasons, and each one pushes the same direction — toward believing differences that are not there.

The distributions are right-skewed with genuine outliers, so the mean describes something nobody experiences. That much is well known and easy to fix by comparing medians. The second and third problems are the ones that actually bite.

What varies, and at which level

Kalibera and Jones's framing is the one I find most useful: treat a measurement as the sum of independent random effects at each level of repetition [1]. For us:

measurement = grand mean + install effect + pass effect + iteration effect

Their central practical claim is that you must find out which level actually carries variance from pilot data rather than assuming, and then spend your repetitions there. Adding iterations when the variance lives at the install level buys almost nothing.

We already know where our variance lives, and it is not the iteration. This fleet has a documented install-parity effect worth roughly ±20% — consecutive installs land on different AOT compile states — and thermal state moved one device +31–49% in a single evening. Both are install- or pass-level effects. Iteration-level noise is small by comparison.

The arithmetic that decides everything

Iterations inside one pass are correlated: thermal drift, page-cache warming and background churn all trend within a run. Kish's design effect quantifies what that costs [5]:

DEFF = 1 + ρ(m − 1) n_effective = n ⁄ DEFF

With m = 50 iterations per pass and an intraclass correlation of only ρ = 0.1, DEFF = 5.9 — fifty iterations carry the information of about eight. At ρ = 0.3, they are worth three. A p-value computed over pooled iterations is therefore roughly six times smaller than it should be, and no quantity of extra iterations repairs it, because the bias is in the structure rather than the sample size.

The consequence for our own data

P17 ran one pass per arm. A cluster-level permutation test on two units has two possible label assignments, which are mirror images of each other, so every arrangement is exactly as extreme as the observed one: the smallest attainable p-value is 1.0. This is not "underpowered" — cluster-level inference is undefined at one cluster per arm, and the same is true of the cluster bootstrap, which degenerates to drawing the same pass forever. Meanwhile our standard 4×50 campaign shape, which we adopted for other reasons, turns out to be the minimum that can clear α = 0.05 at all: four passes per arm give C(8,4) = 70 arrangements and a floor p of 2/70 = 0.029.

Spend the budget on passes, not iterations

If the pass is the unit of evidence, an obvious question follows: given a fixed number of launches, how should they be split between passes and iterations-per-pass? We had been running 4×50. The arithmetic says that is close to the worst available choice.

Under clustering, the variance of an arm decomposes as

Var(arm) = σ²between ⁄ G + σ²within ⁄ (G · n)

for G passes of n iterations. Now hold the total N = G · n fixed. The second term becomes σ²within ⁄ N — a constant, independent of how you split. Only the first term responds. So with a fixed launch budget, precision improves monotonically with more passes, and the iterations inside a pass buy nothing beyond what the fixed total already gives you.

That is not a marginal effect here, because our variance is almost entirely between-pass. Measured on this fleet at 4×50, the between-pass term is 93–99% of the total — on the Galaxy A14, 20.7 against 0.262. Those fifty iterations per pass were diligently shrinking the one percent that does not matter.

Roughly twice the precision for exactly the same number of launches, and the permutation floor collapses along with it. That last row matters as much as the first four: at four passes we can never report p < 0.029 no matter how large the effect, which is precisely why a coherent 17.4% window improvement on the A14 came back unsupported.

Two further effects push the same way and are not in the formula. Install parity is assigned per install and is bimodal, so it only averages down as 1/√G — about 7% residual error on a difference at G = 4, about 3% at G = 20. More passes is the only lever that touches it. And more passes is the only thing that buys inferential resolution at all.

We are moving to 10×20 rather than 20×10. It captures most of the precision at half the installs, twenty iterations keeps per-pass diagnostics readable, and installs are the flakiest step on entry-tier hardware — doubling them doubles our exposure to the install-timeout failures that have already cost several campaign attempts. Pooled p90/p95 support is unaffected by the split, since those are computed across all launches rather than within a pass.

Changing the split closes a series

Run shape is part of the recipe key, so a 10×20 run is not comparable to a banked 4×50 baseline. Re-baselining is a real cost and should be paid deliberately, when the sensitivity gain is worth restarting the series. Archive the old store rather than deleting it: the old runs remain internally valid, and comparing the two shapes on the same devices and versions is a genuine measurement-invariance check — the medians should agree while the intervals tighten. If they do not agree, something in the harness depends on pass length and we need to know before trusting either.

Why the unit of permutation is the whole argument

A permutation test asks: under the null, could these labels have been swapped? The assumption is exchangeability. Iterations from different arms are not exchangeable — they come from different installs, and the install effect is real and large. Permuting iterations tests a null nobody believes and inflates the false-positive rate by roughly the design effect [6,7]. Permuting passes tests the null we actually mean: that a whole run could equally have been labelled either arm.

Effect size, for data that is not normal

Cohen's d assumes normality and equal variances, neither of which holds. Cliff's δ is ordinal — it asks how often a value from one arm exceeds one from the other — so skew and outliers do not distort it [8]. Vargha–Delaney A12 restates the same quantity as a probability of superiority, which I find the most honest thing to report to a non-statistical reader: "a random launch on the new build beats a random launch on the old one 63% of the time." Conventional thresholds put |δ| below 0.147 as negligible [9].

Proving sameness is a different test

Our control-version check — "9.0.0's numbers should not move between measurements" — cannot be done with a significance test. A non-significant result is consistent with no effect and with an effect we lacked the power to see. Equivalence testing (TOST) inverts the null and asks whether the difference is confidently within a margin [10]. Our ±4% noise band is the natural margin, and it must be declared before looking.

Where I think the literature does not fit us

Two places, and both matter more than the choice of test.

Install parity is bimodal, not a variance component

The nested-variance model assumes each level contributes roughly symmetric noise that averaging reduces. Our install-parity effect is not that: it is a two-state switch — the APK lands on speed-profile or verify, and the window moves about 20% accordingly. Averaging over installs does not shrink a bimodal confound the way it shrinks Gaussian noise; with an odd number of installs it simply picks a side. The correct treatment is blocking, not replication: pin the state with pm compile, or balance parities deliberately across arms, and record which state each pass ran in. I have not seen this case addressed in the benchmarking papers, which mostly consider continuous variance sources.

Our dominant risk is confounding, not sampling error

Every method above controls for sampling error. None of them protects against a systematic difference between arms — a warmer device, a different compile state, a background update. Given that our confounds (±20% parity, +31–49% thermal) are larger than most effects we are chasing, design controls do more work than statistics: interleave arms rather than running them in blocks [4], counterbalance order across devices, and record the conditions so a confound can be detected after the fact. A tight confidence interval around a confounded difference is a precise wrong answer.

Power, and the special case of a diluted measure

Sample size scales with the square of the effect you want to see:

n per arm ≈ 2 (zα/2 + zβ)² · CV² ⁄ effect² × DEFF

Halving the detectable effect quadruples the cost. This is why measuring a change through a measure that dilutes it is so expensive — the cost rises with the square of the dilution. Our concrete case: SDK init is 3.8% of end-to-end launch on one device and 1.0% on another, so a 15% init improvement becomes a 0.58% and 0.15% change in TTID.

So TTID cannot serve as evidence that an SDK-init change helped — not for want of effort, but because the effect is below the noise floor before sampling begins, and the confounds are larger still. TTID is a guardrail (did this make things much worse?), never a detector. The question it was standing in for — was the work removed or merely moved past first frame? — should be answered with CPU accounting, which measures the resource directly and varies far less than wall time.

Production is a different problem — and you have a tool the benchmark lacks

The natural reading is that production trades controllable confounds for sample size: you cannot pin the compile state on a stranger's phone, so the best available move is to restrict to a homogeneous slice (one SoC) and let volume smooth the rest. That is half right, and the missing half is the more powerful one.

A staged percentage rollout is randomisation. Randomly assigning devices balances confounders you cannot name, measure, or even suspect — which is strictly stronger than stratifying on the ones you thought of. Stratification then buys precision; randomisation buys validity. They solve different problems and you want both. Two conditions: randomise at the device level rather than the launch, and ensure assignment cannot be self-selected — an opt-in beta cohort is not a random sample.

The formula that governs sample size

For a quantile, the asymptotic sampling variance is

Var(q̂p) ≈ p(1 − p) ⁄ (n · f(qp)²)

The density at the quantile decides everything. Deep in a sparse right tail f is small, so the same n buys far less precision at p99 than at p50 — and the gap widens as the distribution spreads. The spread can be read straight off telemetry as σ = ln(p90/p50) / 1.2816, with no distributional assumption needed to obtain it.

Yes, the distribution matters — in two separate ways

For means, shape governs whether the central limit theorem has taken hold. Berry–Esseen bounds the convergence by the third moment, so the practical requirement scales with skewness squared (rule of thumb n ≥ 25·skew²). Latency is log-normal-ish; at σ = 0.86 the skewness is about 4.5, so a mean needs thousands of samples merely to be approximately normal — and remains outlier-dominated after that. For quantiles, shape enters through f(qp) above: it does not merely scale the answer, it decides which quantiles are affordable at all.

Three ways to make the tail affordable

  • Extreme value theory instead of empirical quantiles. Fit a Generalised Pareto distribution to exceedances above a high threshold and extrapolate. This borrows strength from the whole tail shape rather than the handful of points past p99, and is standard practice in hydrology and finance for precisely this problem.
  • Ask for an exceedance rate, not a quantile. "What share of launches exceed 500 ms" is a binomial proportion — more robust, easier to explain, and usually the real question. Pinning a 1% rate to ±0.5pp needs ~4,600 launches against ~13,500 for p99 at ±5%. The precision is absolute, though: demanding ±0.1pp on a 1% rate costs ~114,000.
  • Pre-period adjustment (CUPED). In a staged rollout every device has pre-exposure launches. Adjusting each device by its own prior behaviour removes variance in proportion to ρ², cutting required sample by the same factor — at ρ = 0.7, roughly half. Given per-device history and a startup counter this is available immediately and costs only analysis.

Clustering does not go away in production

Launches are not independent: the same device, installed apps, storage state and network recur. Effective n is closer to the number of devices than launches — a million launches from 5,000 devices carries roughly 5,000 devices' worth of information about device-level effects, and less if a few heavy users dominate. Compute the ICC across devices before trusting any n, exactly as with passes in the benchmark.

Finally, a rollout is watched continuously, and repeatedly peeking at a fixed-horizon test inflates the false-positive rate badly. Use a sequential procedure — mSPRT or confidence sequences — whose guarantees hold at every stopping time.

What I am changing in our practice

  1. Passes, not iterations — and 10×20 is the new default shape. Four passes per arm is the bare minimum for inference to exist at all; ten is what makes it usable. At a fixed launch budget the within-pass term is a constant, so only the pass count buys precision, and 93–99% of our variance is between-pass. Every pass from an independent install.
  2. Interleave arms rather than running all of A then all of B, so drift cannot align with the comparison.
  3. Report the interval and the effect size, never a bare percentage. A difference without an interval is an anecdote.
  4. Report the minimum detectable effect beside every null result. "No change" is meaningless until paired with "we could only have seen 1.4%".
  5. Clear the noise band as well as the p-value. With large n, trivial differences become significant; ±4% remains the bar for acting.
  6. Declare the family before testing and apply BH-FDR across it [11] — with 17 sections and 4 devices, something will always look significant.
  7. Record compile state and thermal state per pass, so a bimodal confound is visible rather than absorbed into "noise".

Glossary

Plain-language definitions of everything used above. No prior stats background assumed — read this first if any of the terms are unfamiliar, or dip in as you go.

The two things that can be wrong

Variance"noise", "scatter"
How much your measurements bounce around when nothing has actually changed. Run the same build twice and get 48 ms then 53 ms — that spread is variance. More data reduces it.
Bias
A systematic push in one direction, so your answer is consistently off rather than merely scattered. Measuring one build on a hot phone and the other on a cool one biases the comparison. More data does NOT reduce it — you just get a tighter interval around the wrong number.
Confounder
Something other than the thing you are testing that differs between the two groups and could explain the result. Temperature, install state, background apps. Confounders create bias.
Arm
One side of a comparison — "the old build" is one arm, "the new build" the other. Borrowed from clinical trials.

How a startup benchmark is structured

Iteration"launch"
One cold app start, producing one measurement.
Pass
A batch of iterations run back-to-back from a single install. Twenty launches in a row is one pass. The key point of this document is that the pass, not the iteration, is the unit of evidence.
Clustering
When measurements come in groups that resemble each other more than they resemble the rest. Launches inside one pass share a device state, a temperature, an install — so they are clustered, and cannot be treated as independent.
Install paritycompile state
Android may compile an app differently from one install to the next, and the difference is large. It is fixed for a whole pass, so it behaves like a coin flip per install rather than noise that averages out within a pass.

Measuring the damage from clustering

ICCintraclass correlation, ρ (rho)
How alike measurements within a cluster are, from 0 to 1. Near 0 means launches in a pass are as different from each other as from any other pass — clustering costs you nothing. Near 1 means they are near-copies, so fifty launches tell you barely more than one.
Design effectDEFF
How much clustering inflates your uncertainty, calculated as 1 + ρ(m−1) for m iterations per pass. A DEFF of 30 means your interval is far wider than the raw count suggests.
Effective nn_eff
How many genuinely independent measurements you effectively have: the raw count divided by the design effect. Two hundred launches at high ICC can be worth an effective six.

Summarizing a set of numbers

Median
The middle value — half the launches are faster, half slower. Preferred here over the mean because a few extreme launches drag a mean toward a value nobody actually experiences.
Percentile / quantilep90, p95
p90 is the value 90% of launches come in under. It describes the slow tail — the launches users notice and complain about.
Right-skewed
A distribution with a long tail on the slow side. Launches can be arbitrarily slow but never faster than zero, so startup data always looks like this.
CVcoefficient of variation
Spread expressed as a percentage of the typical value, so a 5% CV means the same thing on a 15 ms flagship and a 150 ms budget phone.

Deciding whether a difference is real

Confidence intervalCI
A range of plausible values for the true difference. If it includes zero, "no difference" is still plausible and you have not shown anything.
p-value
The probability of seeing a difference at least this large if the two arms were actually identical. Small means "coincidence is an uncomfortable explanation". It is not the probability that you are right.
Alphasignificance threshold
The p-value cutoff agreed in advance, conventionally 0.05.
Bootstrap
Estimating uncertainty by resampling your own data thousands of times with replacement and watching how much the answer moves. A cluster bootstrap resamples whole passes rather than individual launches, which is what respects the clustering.
Permutation test
Shuffling the "old build" / "new build" labels many times to see how often chance alone produces a gap as big as the one observed. Requires no assumption about the shape of the data.
Floor psmallest attainable p-value
With few clusters there are only so many ways to shuffle the labels, which puts a hard floor under the p-value. With four passes per arm that floor is 0.029 — so a design with fewer passes cannot produce a significant result no matter how large the real effect is.
Statistical power
The chance your experiment detects a real effect of a given size. Conventionally aimed at 80%.
MDEminimum detectable effect
The smallest difference your design could reliably have found. Essential when reporting a null: "no change" means nothing until paired with "and we could only have seen a change of 8% or larger".
Effect sizeCliff's delta, A12
How big the difference is, separate from how confident you are that it exists. A tiny, meaningless difference can be highly significant given enough data.
Equivalence testTOST
The test for actively proving two things are the same within some tolerance. An ordinary test failing to find a difference is not proof of sameness.
Multiple comparisonsBH-FDR
Test enough things and something looks significant by luck. Benjamini–Hochberg adjusts for how many tests you ran, so declare the full list before you start.

Terms that appear in the production section

Randomization
Assigning users or devices to arms by chance. Its power is that it balances confounders you never thought of — which no amount of careful matching can do.
Stratification
Deliberately restricting or balancing on a known factor, such as comparing only one chipset. Improves precision, but only for factors you already knew to worry about.
Exceedance rate
The share of launches slower than some threshold — "3% took over 500 ms". Robust and easy to explain, and much cheaper than an extreme percentile when you have production-scale traffic.
CUPED
Using each device's own before-the-experiment measurements to cancel out its baseline differences, which can cut the required sample size dramatically for free.
EVTextreme value theory, Generalised Pareto
Fitting a curve to the whole tail to estimate extreme percentiles, instead of relying on the handful of data points beyond p99.
Sequential testingmSPRT, always-valid inference
Methods designed to be checked repeatedly as data arrives. Peeking at an ordinary test over and over inflates false positives badly; these do not have that problem.
Central limit theoremCLT
The result that averages tend toward a bell curve as samples grow. It underpins many standard tests, and it arrives slowly for skewed data like ours — one reason to prefer medians and resampling.
