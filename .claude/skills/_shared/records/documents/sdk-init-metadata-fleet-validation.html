Do the new SDK-init attributes work in production?

Every attribute added to the SDK-init span, checked on four physical devices spanning API 29 to 35. Three questions each: does it populate on a real device at all, is the number right when it does, and — the question added in this revision — does the number actually predict a slow init? The three come apart completely. Two attributes have now been deleted, one has been narrowed to fire only when it means something, and exactly one has so far demonstrated predictive power.

Accuracy and usefulness are different properties, and this doc used to conflate them

Everything below the "is the number right?" heading grades accuracy — does the attribute faithfully report the quantity it claims to report. That says nothing about whether the quantity is worth having. thermal-status is the case that proved it: it mirrored the platform perfectly while being nearly useless as an always-on field, because it reports its truth too late to act on. Its accuracy grade alone would have kept it as it was; asking the second question is what drove narrowing it to presence-only, which is why it is now a useful attribute rather than a passing one. An attribute can be flawlessly accurate and carry no information about why an init was slow. The new Does it predict slowness? section asks the second question, and it is the one that should drive keep/remove decisions.

The verdicts

Ordered by what needs a decision, not by attribute order.

Trusted
The triage core is accurate and discriminating.

init-cpu-pct and init-run-delay-pct land within 1–3 points of trace ground truth on every one of 54 graded iterations, and they separate a busy device from a quiet one across a 0→34% induced range. These two carry the ran-slow / was-starved split that the whole attribute set exists to support.

Trusted
init-gc-count is verified on the positive case — as a lower bound.

Graded where collections actually happen: median error 0–1 collections, and it never invents one. It under-counts slightly (mean −0.6 across 30 windows) because the SDK's counter spans a marginally narrower interval than the trace window. Read it as "was GC a competitor", not as an exact count.

Removed
psi-cpu-some-avg10 never populates, on any device, and cannot.

Zero of 12 launches across all four devices — and an AOSP policy review confirmed this is by design, not per-vendor luck. Removed from the codebase. Details below.

Resolved
The two attributes that had never been seen doing their job have since been seen doing it.

Both were open because the presence sweep could not exercise them: seconds-since-install and seconds-since-update were identical on all four devices (every device had a fresh install, so the separation that is their entire purpose never appeared), and low-memory was absent everywhere — correct by design, since it emits only when true, but meaning its emit path had never fired.

X17 drove a real update and the recency pair diverged as designed, verified to +2 s against device ground truth. X15 induced memory pressure and low-memory fired at the platform threshold (147,196 KB against an AOSP-derived 147,456). Neither is speculative any more.

Predictive
thermal-headroom-pct is the one attribute shown to predict a slow init.

Within-phase rank correlation of +0.63 with the init window across 319 paired launches on the flagship, surviving a 10,000-draw permutation test and holding at +0.55 once memory availability is partialled out. Worth roughly +5.5 ms across the headroom range on a 91 ms median. Accuracy was already established; this is the first evidence that the quantity it accurately reports is one worth having.

Removed
init-maj-faults failed both remaining tests and has been deleted.

It discriminated on only two of four devices, and when finally tested for predictive power it returned within-phase rho +0.10 on 319 launches — not significant. init-disk-read-kb answers the same IO question while being graded, directional across the full tier range, and not saturating. Removing it also took /proc/self/stat off the init path entirely.

Unknown
Every other attribute's predictive power is untested, not negative.

The correlation dataset came from a thermal ramp, so it spans temperature and little else. Memory, install recency and prefs size barely varied, and a flat predictor cannot show a relationship whether or not one exists. Absence of correlation here is absence of evidence — the range was never there to test it. Production data, which supplies that range naturally, is where this gets settled.

Also present and stable on every device: 17 per-section duration attributes (the emb-*-duration-ms init breakdown), giving 37 attributes per launch — 36 on the Go device, the one missing entry being thermal headroom.

Three things the matrix says that the grades do not

  • The Pixel 3 is contended even at idle. It is the only device reporting non-zero run-delay (10–17%) on an otherwise quiet launch, with correspondingly lower CPU share (79–88% vs 94–98%). The attribute pair is doing exactly its job: flagging a device that gets starved rather than one that runs slowly.
  • Only the Go device collects garbage during init (5 collections; zero on the other three). That is why the init-gc-count positive case is ungradeable anywhere else — a permanent zero on the flagship is the device, not the attribute.
  • Thermal headroom sits at 55–65% on idle, cool devices (all reporting thermal-status: none). Useful calibration: mid-scale headroom is the resting state, not a warning sign.

Does it predict slowness?

Accuracy is necessary and not sufficient. This section asks the separate question: when an attribute moves, does the init window move with it? Measured on 442 launches that carry both a window duration and a full attribute map in the same record, drawn from the thermal-ramp batch — the only existing dataset where an environmental attribute genuinely varies.

The attributes are three populations, not one

Correlating all of them against the window and reading down the list would produce confident nonsense, because most of them contain the window already.

  • Decompositions. The 17 emb-*-duration-ms values, plus init-cpu-pct and init-run-delay-pct — CPU share literally has wall time in its denominator. These correlate by construction; modules-init-duration-ms comes back at +0.92, which is a sanity check on the method rather than a finding.
  • In-window counters. init-disk-read-kb, init-gc-count, init-maj-faults accumulate during the window, so a longer window mechanically permits more of them. This is the trap that once made a raw interrupt count read +0.64 while the interrupt rate read −0.147 and reversed a conclusion.
  • Exogenous state. Thermal, memory, and recency attributes are fixed before the window opens. Only these support a clean prediction question, and only these are FDR-corrected below.

Method, and the confound it exists to defeat

In a thermal ramp, temperature and elapsed time rise together by design, so a raw correlation cannot distinguish "hot is slow" from "later is slow". Every headline number here is therefore a within-phase rank correlation: both variables are ranked inside each phase, which removes that phase's mean temperature, mean duration, and position in the experiment simultaneously. Significance comes from permuting the attribute within phase (10,000 draws), preserving that structure and breaking only the attribute–duration link. Everything is rank-based because the known thermal response is a threshold — one device was immune below ~35 °C then drifted — and a threshold shows up weakly in any linear coefficient.

The headroom result is not a memory effect in disguise

mem-available-pct came back strong on the same launches, and if free memory and thermal headroom drift together across a ramp the headroom finding could be borrowed. Partial rank correlation settles it: headroom against the window controlling for memory holds at +0.552 (p<0.001), memory controlling for headroom holds at −0.373, and the two predictors are only moderately collinear at −0.369. Both carry independent signal.

Do not read the time-monotonic rows as findings

seconds-since-boot, seconds-since-install, seconds-since-update and version_startup_counter all increase monotonically over an experiment, so in a session where the device is also steadily heating they will track anything else that drifts. Their apparent significance is most plausibly shared drift, not prediction, and the within-phase control cannot fully remove it because these attributes are still monotonic inside a phase. They are listed for completeness and should be treated as untested, exactly like the attributes that did not vary at all.

Three limits on all of the above

  • The reversal control was not available. The batch contains cooling and recovery phases, whose value is that temperature falls while time keeps rising — the cleanest way to separate the two. But only staircase and t10-recovery carried enough launches with both a window and attributes, so the within-phase control rests on two phases rather than five, and the direction-reversal test could not be run.
  • One device carries the result. 319 of the 442 paired launches are the flagship. The Pixel 3 agrees in sign with a larger absolute effect but is underpowered at n=53, and the entry tier cannot contribute at all because the attribute does not exist below API 30. Given that headroom is already known not to be comparable across vendors, a flagship result should not be assumed to transfer.
  • Only temperature was varied. This dataset exists because a thermal ramp was deliberately induced. Every other environmental dimension sat near its modal value, which is why their correlations are uninformative rather than negative.

Why kernel PSI can never work in an app, and what that costs us

The one removal in this set is worth explaining, because the reason generalises to every future idea that starts "we could just read /proc/…".

Apps cannot read kernel Pressure Stall Information, and this is AOSP architecture rather than a per-vendor gap. AOSP gives the pressure files their own SELinux types (proc_pressure_cpu and siblings, via genfscon), so a broad /proc grant does not cover them — every domain needs an explicit allow. Exactly two have one: lmkd, which consumes PSI to drive low-memory kills, and system_server. There is no grant in untrusted_app.te, priv_app.te, or domain.te, and SELinux is default-deny, so the missing grant is the block. That has held from Android 10 — when the types were introduced — through current master, with no OEM, LineageOS, or GrapheneOS policy found that widens it. Apps receive only the derived signal, second-hand, via onTrimMemory.

The trap: a shell test says "supported" and is wrong

adb shell cat /proc/pressure/cpu succeeds on all four devices — the shell domain keeps broad legacy /proc access an app never gets. The only valid test of app-side readability is adb shell run-as <debuggable.pkg> cat /proc/pressure/cpu, which returns Permission denied. Combined with a silent runCatching{}.getOrNull() around the read, an attribute like this looks merely rare rather than dead — it emitted nothing, 12 times out of 12, without a single error anywhere.

The cost: there is no app-readable device-wide CPU-contention signal at all. Own-thread run-delay carries that diagnostic class alone, uncorroborated. That is tolerable precisely because run-delay is the best-verified attribute in the set — within 0–2 points of trace truth across all 54 graded iterations — but it does mean a high run-delay reading cannot be cross-checked against an independent device-wide view, and never will be.

Confidence: high, not absolute. This is inference from the shared AOSP base policy plus CTS/VTS enforcement pressure, not a document certifying every OEM. No counterexample was found. Testing further vendors with the run-as command above would corroborate it; a successful read on any shipping device would overturn it.

One measurement caveat that applies to the whole grading effort

Attributes were read through the in-app verification tap, not from cached payloads. This is not a convenience: exporters never see the sdk-init span — it is marked emb.private and filtered out — and cached-payload pulls need both a debuggable build and a foreground user session, silently returning zero-span payloads otherwise. The tap is the only channel that observes this span on an unattended device.

What changed as a result

psi-cpu-some-avg10 was removed from SdkInitAttributeKeys and SdkInitEnvironmentAttributes, with the reason recorded in the init-run-delay-pct kdoc so it is not re-proposed — deleting it silently would have invited exactly that. Separately, seconds-since-boot was corrected to measure awake time and moved to the environment attributes.

Two further changes followed the predictive analysis:

  • init-maj-faults deleted. It saturated at both ends of the device range, emitted a genuine 0 on most inits — which reads as "no storage IO occurred" even when init-disk-read-kb on the same span is non-zero — and showed no predictive power. It was the only consumer of /proc/self/stat, so its removal also takes two procfs reads off the init path along with the field-counting needed to skip a process name that may contain spaces and parentheses.
  • thermal-status narrowed to presence-only. It now emits only when the status is not none, following the same idiom as low-memory: absence means a healthy device, presence means the device was throttling. It was previously a constant string on nearly every init span. The attribute is kept rather than deleted because it remains the calibrated signal at and beyond the severe threshold, where headroom's scale explicitly is not.
    Implementation note: the headroom block deliberately remains a sibling of the status branch rather than nesting inside it. Status is none in the common case, which is exactly when headroom is the attribute worth having, so nesting or an early return would silently drop the useful signal precisely where it matters. A test pins headroom present while status is none.

No change was made to thermal-headroom-pct, and the analysis above is the justification: it is the only attribute in the set with demonstrated predictive validity, so it earns its place on every span that can carry it.

Outstanding work lives in the tracker, not here

Everything this report leaves unresolved — grading the IO pair, making low-memory fire, exercising the install-vs-update separation, and settling the thermal questions — is tracked as X14–X17 in the startup improvements tracker, each with a linked experiment design. The tracker is the single source of truth for outstanding work; this document is a record of what was measured and concluded. Two lists of pending work inevitably drift, and then neither can be trusted.

What this does not cover

  • Accuracy on the flagship and the Pixel 3. Both were checked for population only. The graded accuracy figures come from the A14 and the A01; nothing here says the numbers are right on the other two, only that they exist and look plausible.
  • Run-delay across a wide range on the Go device. Eight CPU hogs there produced only 1–5% run-delay — execution slowed without queueing — so the wide-range validation rests on the A14's 0→34% span alone.
  • Real production conditions. Every figure here comes from a debug build on a developer-controlled device. Build type changes behaviour materially: GC prevalence during init on the Go device differs sharply between the debug and benchmark builds.
  • Anything about attribute cost. This report covers correctness and availability, not the overhead of collecting them.
  • Predictive power for anything except temperature. The correlation dataset was a thermal ramp. Memory pressure, install recency, prefs size and contention did not meaningfully vary, so nothing here tests whether those attributes predict a slow init. A flat predictor cannot demonstrate a relationship, and its silence is not evidence against one.
  • Whether any of this holds in production. Everything above is a developer-controlled device running a manufactured thermal ramp. Production supplies the range of conditions naturally, across vendors and tiers that will never be on a desk here, and that is where the predictive question gets its real answer.
