Reading the changes together

9.2.0 is the headline: the largest single step in the series, at every statistic, on both devices measured so far. On the flagship it lands 17.16 → 11.50 ms at the median (−33%) with p90 and p95 moving in near-lockstep (−30%, −29%) — for scale, the entire 8.3.0 → 9.1.0 arc bought −18% on that device. The number arrives triple-checked: two days before release the same commit measured 12.00 ms as a tapped snapshot, subtracting the tap's independently measured +0.38 ms cost predicts ~11.6, and the released untapped build ingested at 11.50 — three campaigns, two build provenances, agreement within 0.1 ms. But the step's size is device-dependent, and the attribution question sharpened twice in one morning: on the common 9.0.0 → 9.2.0 step, three of four devices cluster tightly — flagship −38.1%, A14 −35.3%, A01 Core −35.1% — while the Pixel 3 alone lags at −25.2%. ANSWERED 2026-08-25 (X35): the Pixel 3's shortfall is concentrated in one phase — emb-post-services-setup improved −67% there against −84% on its peers, accounting for ~79% of its ~2.6 ms deficit. Containment was measured rather than assumed (24 traces): modules-init is a parent of config-service-init, span-service-init and others, while post-services-setup is an independent sibling — and inside that parent the Pixel 3 actually did better than its peers. (That containment was measured on 9.2.0 traces only; the 9.0.0-side structure was filled in on 2026-08-26 by X38, which found modules-init's child set entirely unmatched by name across the step — so its cross-version duration comparison is sound, but its contents are not like-for-like. The finding above is unaffected, because it rests on post-services-setup, a sibling.) That argues against the ART-generation hypothesis in its global form: uniformly weaker codegen would depress every section, but half of them improved more on the Pixel 3. Full analysis: claude-output/2026-08-25-x35-attribution-answer.md. With the entry tier landed, the Pixel 3 is unambiguously the outlier rather than the flagship being special, which is the cleaner form of the question: what does 9.2.0 improve that three devices get and the 2018 Pixel does not? The ART-generation split is the first hypothesis to test — the Pixel 3 is the fleet's oldest OS, and the three that cluster span flagship, mid and Go tiers, so tier and CPU class are ruled out as the discriminator. Note the engine flip cannot be the explanation: it defaults off at HEAD and X32 measured it at −4.3% on the flagship, far too small and never shipped. Section-level attribution for 9.2.0 is the open analysis.

In absolute terms the entry tier is the headline: 50 ms of main-thread blocking removed from the Go device (142.69 → 92.58 ms), against 7 ms on the flagship. The tail moved with it — p90 −27.7%, p95 −20.3% — so the improvement reaches the slow launches that users actually notice, on the device where they hurt most. The p95 improving less than the median (−20.3% vs −35.1%) says the worst launches on that device retain a floor the release did not touch, which is consistent with its outliers being device-owned (GC, install aftermath, storage) rather than SDK-owned.

9.2.0 is supported everywhere, at every statistic: all 22 of its pairwise comparisons across the four devices have intervals excluding zero, nearly all at the design floor of 5×10⁻⁵. That uniformity is unusual in this series and is the strongest single result in it — the release improves the median, p90 and p95 on the flagship, both mid devices and the Go device, with no arm dissenting. Its smallest step is the Pixel 3's (−15.5% median) and its largest in absolute terms is the A01's (−50.11 ms).

Direction first: of the 21 pre-9.2.0 point estimates across median, p90 and p95, twenty are improvements — monotonic 8.3.0 → 9.0.0 → 9.1.0 → 9.2.0 at every statistic on every device that completed — and the single positive (A14 p95, 8.3.0 → 9.0.0, +3.23 ms) carries p = 0.70: noise by every test, reported rather than hidden. The tails move with the medians on every device, which rules out the classic failure mode where a median improvement is bought by making the bad launches worse; if anything the flagship's tail improves slightly more gently than its median (−37% p95 vs −45% median from 8.3.0 to 9.2.0), a shape consistent with the residual tail being owned by the device (scheduling, thermal) rather than by SDK work.

Support then falls along the expected power gradient, and that gradient is a property of the statistics, not of the effects. The same seven comparisons grade as: median — five supported, two suggestive; p90 — five supported, one suggestive, one not; p95 — three supported, one suggestive, three not. Order statistics deep in the noisy region need more data than centres do, so support thinning from median → p90 → p95 at a fixed sample size is exactly what real, consistent effects look like; contradiction would look like sign flips, and there are none above noise. One instructive inversion: the A14's 8.3.0 → 9.1.0 is only suggestive at the median but supported at p90 (−9.53 ms, CI [−15.23, −3.49], p = 0.004) — with between-pass variance as large as that device's, which statistic clears the bar can come down to where the variance happens to concentrate.

Device by device: the Pixel 3 is the precision instrument — five of its six tested tail comparisons sit at or near the design's floor of 5×10⁻⁵, and its one failure (p95 of the tiny 8.3.0 → 9.0.0 step, −0.94 ms) is a real effect too small for that statistic. The flagship's testable pairs all support; its 9.1.0-involving pairs are level-only because that arm's n=198 blocks positional pass recovery. The A14 is the wide-variance device: medians and the p90 endpoint grade, everything deeper in its tail does not — a power statement, not an SDK statement. 9.2.0 so far: every landed level continues the improvement at every statistic (flagship −45 / −39 / −37 % for median / p90 / p95 from 8.3.0; Pixel 3 −27 / −25 / −23 %), and its significance tests run at the next ingest gap so the bootstrap never shares the host with a live leg. Several p-values in these tables sit at 5×10⁻⁵ — the 4-pass designs this series replaced had a floor of 0.029, so this entire level of support was structurally unreportable before the shape change.

How to read every number here

The metric of record is the SDK init blocking window — the quantity the SDK owns and the one a customer feels as "the SDK is holding up my startup". TTID is deliberately not used as the arbiter: its minimum detectable effect on this fleet (~80 ms) is ~5× larger than anything SDK init can produce, so TTID flatness is guaranteed by design and carries no information (the full demonstration is in Appendix A). Medians, not means — these distributions are right-skewed and a mean describes something no launch experiences. Inference always treats the pass as the unit of evidence: 93–99% of an arm's variance on this fleet is between passes, so launch-level tests would invent precision.

The improvement is measured against a rising baseline of value. What the SDK does during init grows with nearly every release — 9.x captures meaningfully more than 8.3.0 — so window reductions here are cost falling while capability rises. The placement audit (Appendix A) further showed the 8.3.0 → 9.1.0 reduction moved work off the main thread rather than deleting it, with both off-window main-thread checks flat; an equivalent audit for 9.2.0 has not yet run.

Caveats — current series

Window source: pre-9.2.0 versions lack emb-sdk-start, so windows are the composed reconstruction (modules-init start → post-services-setup end), which equals the sdk-init span interval by construction; ingest uses the same instrument for 9.2.0 for series consistency. · Pass count is series-defining: the shape change from 4×50 to 10×20 moved medians device-dependently (Pixel 3 −10 to −19%, A14 +10 to +17%) for reasons that remain unexplained after a 1,000-trace decomposition — within-pass drift is real (Pixel 3 heats, A14 warms up, mechanism confirmed via in-window CPU time) but explains only 7–37% of the shift, and the old-vs-new comparison is confounded with run date; settling it is tracker X28. Never read this series' absolute values against any other shape, including Appendix A. · The A14 alternates fast/slow install-parity states in every version; ten passes per arm represent both states. · 8.3.0 required compat patches to three example screens (API breaks); MainApplication untouched, so init comparability holds. · 9.2.0 legs run from a worktree pinned to the released artifact on mavenCentral, untapped, gates verified off.

Where the work went: off the main thread, not away250-trace placement audit, 8.3.0 → 9.1.0

A per-trace CPU audit showed the window reduction is a change of placement, not of total cost: main-thread CPU from process start to TTID barely moved (A01 −2.1%, A14 −1.4%) while whole-process pre-TTID CPU rose 6–11% — work shifting to background threads, e.g. the config decode moving to a worker in 9.0. Two checks ruled out the bad versions of that: main thread running time outside the window was flat (+1.6 ms), so work was not deferred back onto the main thread; and main-thread blocked time outside the window was flat (~339 ms), so the reduction was not bought by making the main thread wait on newly-backgrounded work — the failure mode a CPU-only audit is structurally blind to. Standing rule this established: a window-only improvement is cost re-attribution until placement is checked; every future window claim (9.2.0's included) owes this audit.
